# Escalation taxonomy v1 — how arguments become violence
Status: DRAFT FRAMEWORK (hypotheses to test, not findings) · 2026-11-08
Companion: `circumstance-schema-v1.md` · Dashboard: Research tab

## The question
Most gun violence is not a bolt from nowhere. The research question is: what
turns an ordinary conflict into a shooting — and where, exactly, does it become
hard to stop? This taxonomy gives the circumstance database a way to code that
question, incident by incident, so patterns can be counted instead of assumed.

## The pathway model
Every violent incident is coded as a sequence, not a single cause. Stages:

1. **Encounter** — who meets whom, where, with what history. (Strangers,
   acquaintances, family, rivals; prior grievance or none.)
2. **Trigger** — the event that starts the conflict. See trigger types below.
3. **Interpretation** — what each side thinks the trigger meant. This is
   where misunderstandings live. See misunderstanding types below.
4. **Challenge / response** — the exchange that follows: words, gestures,
   threats, displays. Status is usually what is being negotiated here, even
   when the words are about something else.
5. **Escalation** — the exchange intensifies. Each step narrows the exits.
6. **Point of no return** — the moment after which stopping takes an outside
   intervention (a weapon is drawn, a third party joins, someone is hurt).
7. **Violence** — the shooting itself.
8. **Aftermath** — flight, retaliation risk, silence. (Retaliation is often
   the trigger of the *next* incident — the database links them.)

Most incidents never pass stage 4. The database exists to find out which
combinations of trigger + misunderstanding + context do pass it, and how often.

## Trigger types (what started it)
- **Status challenge** — insult, disrespect, public slight, challenge to
  reputation. The most common expressive trigger in the literature.
- **Resource dispute** — money, property, territory, drugs, a debt.
- **Relationship / jealousy** — partner, ex-partner, rival, family conflict.
- **Prior grievance / retaliation** — payback for an earlier incident.
- **Instrumental objective** — robbery, theft, or violence used to get
  something (planned, not reactive).
- **Mistaken identity / wrong target** — the victim was not the intended one.
- **Chaos / accident** — no clear trigger in the record; coded as such, not
  forced into a category.

## Misunderstanding types (what was misread) — the innovative core
These are the heart of the database. Each is a falsifiable claim about what
went wrong in stage 3:

1. **Misread intent (hostile attribution)** — a neutral or ambiguous act is
   read as a threat. The literature's most robust finding: people primed by
   prior violence read hostility into noise. Test: does the record show an
   ambiguous opening move (a look, a gesture, a car passing slowly)?
2. **Misread words** — an insult was not intended, or was intended as a joke,
   a test, or talk — but landed as a challenge. Test: do accounts disagree
   about what was said?
3. **Misread audience** — the conflict changes because others are watching.
   Backing down in front of peers costs status; the same words alone would
   have ended at stage 4. Test: were peers / bystanders present, and did the
   exchange change when they arrived?
4. **Misread capability** — a weapon's presence changes what each side thinks
   the other will do, and shortens the time to act ("if I don't, he will").
   Test: when did the weapon appear in the sequence — before or after the
   challenge?
5. **Misread relationship** — strangers treated as rivals, or acquaintances
   treated as strangers. Test: did the parties know each other, and did they
   act as if they did?
6. **No face-saving exit** — neither side could stop without losing face, and
   no third party offered one. This is not a misunderstanding of fact; it is a
   structural trap. Test: was there a moment a third party could have
   intervened, and did anyone try?

## Escalation mechanisms (what speeds it up)
- Audience / peer presence · Alcohol or drugs · Group vs. individual ·
  Prior history between the parties · A weapon already visible ·
  Time pressure (a car leaving, a crowd forming) · A prior incident the same day.
Each is coded as present / absent / unknown — never inferred.

## De-escalation points (where it could have stopped)
Every stage has an exit, and the database records which exits existed:
- Stage 3: a clarifying response ("what did you mean by that?") — rare, but
  it happens, and counting it matters.
- Stage 4: a third party stepping in, one side leaving, a joke that lands.
- Stage 5: interruption — police, family, a crowd dispersing.
- Stage 6: almost none — this is the honest finding the taxonomy expects:
  past the point of no return, only outside force stops it.
Prevention intelligence is built from these points: an intervention that only
works at stage 3 is a different product from one that works at stage 5.

## How it maps to motive (signal, never label)
- Status challenge + misread intent/audience → **expressive-leaning signal**.
- Instrumental objective + planned steps → **instrumental-leaning signal**.
- Retaliation → expressive-leaning (it is about a prior wrong, not a gain),
  but coded separately because retaliation is also a *link* between incidents.
- Mistaken identity / chaos → **indeterminate** until reviewed.
- Anything with missing circumstance data → **unknown**, always visible as
  its own share. Unknown is a finding, not a gap to fill with guessing.

## Grounding (what this is built on)
- Luckenbill (1977): homicide as a situated transaction — a sequence of
  moves, not a trait. The stage model follows him.
- Felson & Tedeschi: the social interactionist account — violence as a
  response to perceived rule-breaking, especially around status.
- Collins: confrontational tension — most confrontations do *not* become
  violence; the question is what lets tension cross the line.
- Athens: violentization — how people learn violence as a response over time.
- Katz ("righteous slaughter"): violence experienced by the actor as morally
  required in the moment — which is why "misunderstanding" is the right unit
  of analysis, not "evil intent."
- The dashboard's own sources: Cornell (circumstance categories map to groups
  of crimes), University of Chicago Crime Lab, Princeton gun-violence
  research, Sampson & Laub (1993).

## What would prove this wrong (test plan)
- If reviewed cases show most shootings have no stage 3–4 exchange at all
  (purely instrumental, no interaction), the pathway model is wrong for those
  cases and the taxonomy splits: interactional vs. non-interactional.
- If "misread intent" cannot be coded reliably from records (reviewers
  disagree more than they agree), that category is dropped or narrowed.
- If the expressive/instrumental split does not predict anything about
  escalation (same pathways, same speed), the split is descriptive only and
  the taxonomy says so.
