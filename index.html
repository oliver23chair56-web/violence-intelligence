The shape of the problem is not what most people assume. In 2020, of 44,610 firearm deaths, suicide was 24,292 (54.5%) and homicide 19,383 (43.4%); accidental 535 and undetermined 400 made up the rest. Suicide has been the majority of U.S. firearm deaths in every year of this series — 16,599 of 28,575 in 1999 (58.1%). Homicide is the more volatile series: flat near 11,000–12,000 through 2014, then 12,974 (2015) → 14,542 (2017) → 19,383 (2020, +33% in one year). Total firearm deaths rose from 28,575 (1999) to 44,610 (2020, +56%).

Two systems, shown separately. WONDER counts deaths on death certificates; the FBI series above counts offenses reported by police. They do not cover the same universe — e.g. WONDER records 12,974 firearm homicides in 2015 against the FBI's 15,643 homicide offenses — so the two are never merged into one number. Both are shown because each answers a different question: how many people died, and how many offenses were reported.

National expansion: data-readiness before rankings

The platform is designed to expand from Chicago to ten consistently selected U.S. cities. A defensible top-ten list is not yet populated: comparing agencies without matching population, offense definitions and reporting coverage would produce misleading rankings.

01 · Define the population

Cities above a predeclared population threshold; distinguish municipal city boundaries from police-agency service areas.

02 · Compare like with like

Use one calendar year, FBI violent-crime offense definitions, valid annual population denominators and verified agency reporting coverage.

03 · Rank and verify

Publish rates per 100,000, months reported, sources and missingness. The actual top ten will be computed only after data-quality checks.

Current verified national city rankings in this application: none. Chicago is the first local research environment, not an assertion that it ranks in the national top ten. National totals above come from the FBI Crime Data Explorer; city-level rates still need the same coverage checks.

National firearm-mortality trends (CDC WONDER) are next: the API is verified working and a multi-year query is in flight. State-level series need the CDC NVSS mortality files, since the WONDER API is national-only.

Expressive vs. instrumental: a research signal, not a label

The platform's core research question is whether circumstance patterns can inform prevention. Expressive violence is driven by interpersonal conflict, revenge, or anger. Instrumental violence is planned to obtain something else: money, status, or power. No case on this dashboard has been assigned one of these labels. Circumstance codes are a research signal — a reason to look closer — never a determination of motive. The boundary between the two is fluid, and people move between categories over time.

Mapping rule used here (signal only): argument / conflict / retaliation codes point toward expressive; robbery / felony-for-gain codes point toward instrumental; gang-associated codes are not automatically either. Every mapping below preserves the original code label and the share that is unknown.

How arguments escalate — a framework to test, not a finding

Most shootings are not a bolt from nowhere. The research question is what turns an ordinary conflict into violence — and where, exactly, it becomes hard to stop. This framework codes each incident as a sequence, so patterns can be counted instead of assumed. It is a hypothesis. The database exists to test it.

The pathway (8 stages)

1 Encounter — who meets whom, where, with what history  →  2 Trigger — the event that starts it  →  3 Interpretation — what each side thinks it meant (where misunderstandings live)  →  4 Challenge / response — the exchange, usually about status even when the words are about something else  →  5 Escalation — each step narrows the exits  →  6 Point of no return — after this, only outside intervention stops it  →  7 Violence  →  8 Aftermath — flight, and retaliation risk, which is often the trigger of the next incident.

Most incidents never pass stage 4. The database will show which combinations of trigger + misunderstanding + context do pass it, and how often.

Six misunderstandings that turn conflict violent

MisunderstandingWhat was misread
Misread intentA neutral or ambiguous act is read as a threat. People primed by prior violence read hostility into noise.
Misread wordsAn insult was not intended — a joke, a test, talk — but landed as a challenge. Accounts of what was said disagree.
Misread audienceOthers are watching. Backing down in front of peers costs status; the same words alone would have ended it.
Misread capabilityA weapon's presence changes what each side thinks the other will do — "if I don't, he will" — and shortens the time to act.
Misread relationshipStrangers treated as rivals, or acquaintances treated as strangers.
No face-saving exitNeither side could stop without losing face, and no third party offered one. A structural trap, not a misread fact.

Where it could have stopped

Every stage has an exit: a clarifying response at stage 3; a third party stepping in, or one side leaving, at stage 4; interruption at stage 5. Past stage 6, almost none — that is the honest finding this framework expects. Prevention is built from these points: an intervention that works at stage 3 is a different product from one that works at stage 5.

Grounded in: Luckenbill (1977, homicide as a situated transaction); Felson & Tedeschi (violence as response to perceived rule-breaking); Collins (confrontational tension); Athens (violentization); Katz ("righteous slaughter"). Full framework: research/escalation-taxonomy-v1.md in the project repository.

Methods & coverage

Dataset: Chicago Violence Reduction — Victims. Grain: one row per gunshot victimization. Coverage: 2020 and 2022–2025 historical snapshots; 2021 was not imported. No motive classifications have been reviewed or published (0 reviewed motives). 2025 is the most recent snapshot and may be revised by the source. Counts are reported victimizations, not confirmed shootings, distinct victims, or adjudicated incidents. No incident-level maps, no personal identifiers, no predictive policing, no person-level risk scores. Raw circumstance tables are not published; only aggregated, anonymized breakdowns appear here.

What would make this stronger next: import 2021; reconcile the state-table denominators against the Illinois State Police and FBI sources; add reviewed case narratives with human approval before any motive label is published; extend the same grain and definitions to additional cities before any ranking.
